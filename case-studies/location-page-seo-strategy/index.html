Location Page SEO Strategy

Plan genuine location pages that serve real local demand

What Is Location Page SEO Strategy?

A location page strategy decides which towns and neighborhoods around a dental practice deserve their own page on the website, and what each of those pages should say. It is not a trick for ranking in every zip code. It is a plan for matching real patient searches, like "dentist in [town]", to a page that genuinely helps someone in that area.

Done well, each location page explains how the practice serves that community: drive times, parking, transit or landmarks, which services patients from there usually need, and details only this office can offer. For a multi-office group, each office gets its own page tied to its own Google listing.

Done poorly, it becomes dozens of near-identical pages with only the city name swapped. Google tends to treat those as thin, and patients see that nothing on the page is specific to them. The strategy is about choosing fewer pages and making each one earn its place. It works alongside dental local SEO and the Google Business Profile.

Why It Matters for a Dental Practice

Patients search by place

Many dental searches include a town, a neighborhood, or "near me." A page that clearly covers an area gives search engines and patients a direct answer to that question.

Distance limits who will come

Most patients will not drive far for routine care. Pages should reflect the areas the practice can realistically serve, not every town on a map.

Supports the map listing

Your Google Business Profile links to one page. A strong, consistent location page gives that listing a trustworthy landing spot and reinforces your address and service area.

Helps patients decide

Directions, parking, hours, and a note on what the neighborhood's patients often need make it easier to book. Generic text does none of that.

Protects against thin content

Pages that only swap city names add little value and can dilute the site. Planning fewer, better pages avoids that risk.

Scales for multiple offices

Groups with several locations need a clean structure so each office is findable, and so pages do not compete with each other for the same searches.

Step-by-Step: What a Dental SEO Consultant Checks

This is the order a dental SEO consultant works through when planning location pages for a practice. Each step builds on the one before it.

1. Map the real service area

Start with patient data if available: zip codes of current patients, drive-time radius, nearby competitors, and natural barriers like highways or rivers. The aim is to list where patients realistically come from.

2. Check search demand by area

Review keyword tools and Search Console for town and neighborhood queries. Note which areas show real searches, and which are too small or too far to justify a page.

3. Decide which areas get pages

Pick a short list. Typically the office's own city, plus a few neighboring communities with demand and genuine access. Others are better covered in a service-area section on one page.

4. Plan unique content per page

For each page, list what is true only for that area: landmarks, travel routes, parking, community involvement, and common patient needs. Avoid copying paragraphs from page to page.

5. Verify NAP and map details

Confirm name, address, and phone match the Google listing and major directories exactly. Embed a map, add directions, and check hours and accessibility details are accurate.

6. Link to services and the listing

Connect each location page to relevant service pages, and link from services back where it helps. Make sure the Google Business Profile points to the right page for that office.

7. Add structure and conversions

Check titles, headings, schema markup, mobile layout, and click-to-call or booking buttons. A page that is hard to use on a phone loses patients even if it ranks.

8. Set up tracking and review cycles

Tag calls, form fills, and direction requests by page. Schedule reviews to prune, merge, or expand pages based on performance, not guesswork.

What Happens If You Skip It

Skipping this planning usually leads to a handful of predictable problems on the site.

Thin duplicate pages

Dozens of pages with the same text and a different city name give visitors nothing new. Search engines may ignore them or treat the site as low quality.

Pages for areas you cannot serve

Ranking for a town an hour away brings inquiries that never convert. It wastes effort and can confuse patients about where you actually practice.

Mismatched address details

If the page, the Google listing, and directories show different names or phone numbers, it weakens trust signals and confuses both patients and search engines.

Pages competing with each other

Several pages targeting the same search can split visibility, so none of them performs well. A clear structure prevents this.

No clear path to book

A location page with no obvious call, directions, or appointment option sends interested visitors back to search results.

No way to learn what works

Without page-level tracking, you cannot tell which areas bring patients, so decisions about new pages rely on hunches.

My Approach

My approach favors fewer, stronger pages built from real patient and demand data.

  1. Start with evidence

    I review patient zip codes, search demand, and competitor coverage before recommending any page, so the plan reflects reality rather than a template.

  2. Write for the community

    Each page brief lists local details the practice can verify, so the finished page reads as specific to that area.

  3. Keep the structure simple

    I map location pages to services and listings in one clear hierarchy, which makes the site easier to manage and expand.

  4. Involve the practice

    Owners and office managers know their neighborhoods. I ask for their input and confirm every factual detail before publishing.

  5. Review and adjust

    After launch, I look at performance and recommend whether to expand, merge, or retire pages.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many location pages does a practice need?

Usually fewer than people expect. One page per physical office, plus a small number for nearby communities with real demand and access. Anything beyond that should be justified by search data and unique content, not by a desire to cover every town.

Can I just copy a page and change the city name?

That is the pattern to avoid. Pages that differ only by city name tend to be thin and add little for patients. Each page should include details specific to that area, such as directions, landmarks, and patient needs you can verify.

Do I need a page for a town where I have no office?

Only if patients there realistically come to you and there is genuine search demand. Otherwise, a service-area mention on your main location page is usually better than a stand-alone page that offers nothing unique.

How do location pages relate to my Google listing?

The listing should link to the page for that office, and the details must match exactly. The page supports the listing with fuller information, while the listing handles map visibility, reviews, and quick actions like calls and directions.

How long before location pages show results?

It varies with competition, site strength, and how much is already in place. I would not give a fixed timeline. We set a baseline, track changes monthly, and review at set intervals so you see what is and is not working.
