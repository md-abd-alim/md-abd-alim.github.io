Technical SEO Audit & Action Plan

Find the technical issues holding a website back and fix them in order

What Is Technical SEO Audit & Action Plan?

A technical SEO audit is a structured review of how well search engines can find, read, and understand a dental website. It covers crawling, indexing, site speed, mobile behavior, security, page markup, and the errors that quietly hold good content back.

The audit does not judge your writing or your brand. It checks the foundation underneath: whether Google can reach your treatment pages, whether duplicate versions of the site compete with each other, whether pages load quickly on a phone, and whether the markup tells search engines who and where you are.

The result is a prioritized action plan, so your web developer or marketing team knows what to fix first and why. It often sits alongside a broader dental SEO audit, and it protects the investment you make in content and local work.

Why It Matters for a Dental Practice

Pages must be found to rank

If search engines cannot crawl or index a page, it cannot appear in results, no matter how good it is. Technical problems can hide important treatment pages.

Many dental sites have hidden errors

Template changes, redesigns, and plugin updates often leave behind broken links, wrong redirects, or pages blocked from search without anyone noticing.

Patients search on phones

Most people looking for a dentist are on mobile. Slow, cramped, or hard-to-tap pages lose visitors before they ever call.

Duplicate versions split signals

The same site reachable at several addresses, such as with and without www or on HTTP, can dilute signals and confuse which version should appear.

Fixes make other work pay off

Content, links, and Google listing work all depend on a sound site. Repairing the base lets those efforts work as intended.

Priorities save budget

A ranked list of issues keeps developer time on changes that matter, instead of chasing every minor warning a tool reports.

Step-by-Step: What a Dental SEO Consultant Checks

This is roughly the order I follow, moving from whether the site can be seen at all to the finer points of markup and speed.

1. Check crawling and indexation

Compare what the site contains with what Google has indexed. Look for important pages that are missing, thin or unwanted pages that are included, and noindex tags or blocks applied by mistake.

2. Review robots.txt and sitemap

Confirm robots.txt does not block key folders or resources, and that the XML sitemap lists only live, canonical, indexable pages. Make sure it is submitted in Search Console and kept current.

3. Test HTTPS and duplicate versions

Load the HTTP, HTTPS, www, and non-www versions of the site. Only one should resolve, with the others redirecting to it in a single step, and no mixed-content warnings on secure pages.

4. Audit canonicals and redirects

Check that each page points to the right canonical address, and look for redirect chains, loops, and temporary redirects that should be permanent. Trailing slashes and parameter URLs often cause trouble.

5. Measure speed and Core Web Vitals

Review real-user and lab data for loading, interactivity, and layout stability on key pages. Identify the causes, such as oversized images, heavy scripts, or slow hosting, rather than just reporting scores.

6. Evaluate mobile usability

View key pages on actual phones. Check readable text, tap targets, sticky call buttons, forms, pop-ups that block content, and whether mobile pages match desktop content.

7. Review titles, headings, and schema

Look for missing, duplicated, or truncated title tags, a clear heading structure, and valid structured data such as dental business, location, and FAQ markup that matches what is visible on the page.

8. Find broken links and build the plan

Crawl for 404 errors, broken internal links, and orphan pages. Then rank every finding by impact and effort, and write the fix plan with owners and a re-check date.

What Happens If You Skip It

Technical issues rarely announce themselves. They tend to appear as flat traffic, missing pages, or rankings that never quite move.

Pages that never get indexed

A stray noindex tag or blocked folder can keep a service page out of search for months, while the practice assumes the content is simply not working.

Split authority across URLs

When several versions of the site respond, links and signals spread across them instead of strengthening one address.

Slow pages lose patients

Heavy images and scripts make pages sluggish on phones. Visitors leave before they read, and the page may be held back in search too.

Redesigns that damage rankings

Launching a new site without mapping old URLs to new ones can produce broken pages and lost visibility that take time to recover.

Wasted content spend

New pages and blog posts add little if the site has crawl or indexing problems that stop search engines from reading them.

Misleading or broken markup

Schema that does not match the page, or errors in structured data, can cause rich results to be ignored and may raise trust concerns.

My Approach

I keep technical audits practical. The goal is a list your developer can act on, not a long report of warnings.

  1. Verify with several sources

    I combine a site crawl, Search Console data, and manual checks, so one tool's false alarm does not drive decisions.

  2. Rank by patient impact

    Issues that affect treatment pages, location pages, and booking paths come before cosmetic warnings.

  3. Explain in plain language

    Each finding states what is wrong, why it matters, and what the fix looks like, so owners and developers both understand it.

  4. Respect your platform

    Recommendations fit your actual CMS and theme, since a fix that suits WordPress may not suit another system.

  5. Re-check after changes

    I recommend verifying fixes after release so a new error is caught before it becomes a long-term problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should a dental website have a technical audit?

A full audit makes sense before a redesign, after a migration, or when traffic drops without explanation. Otherwise, many practices benefit from a lighter check once or twice a year, plus monitoring in Search Console so new errors surface quickly.

Will my developer need to make the fixes?

Often yes, for items like redirects, speed, and schema. Simple changes such as titles and some sitemap settings may be handled inside your CMS. I write the plan so each task is clear enough to hand to whoever manages your site.

Is a good PageSpeed score enough?

Not by itself. Scores are one signal, and lab results can differ from what real visitors experience. I look at field data, the specific causes of slowness, and how key pages behave on actual phones before recommending changes.

Do I need schema markup on my dental site?

Schema is not a requirement for ranking, but accurate markup helps search engines understand your practice, address, and services. It must match what is visible on the page. Incorrect or exaggerated markup can do more harm than leaving it out.

Can technical fixes guarantee higher rankings?

No. Fixes remove barriers and give good content a fair chance, but rankings depend on competition, relevance, and many other factors. I treat the audit as a way to reduce risk and improve the foundation, not as a promise of position.
